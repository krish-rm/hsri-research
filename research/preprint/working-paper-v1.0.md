## Abstract
As frontier artificial intelligence systems scale toward recursive algorithmic capability, cognitive parity, and potential superintelligence (ASI), international governance frameworks remain overwhelmingly fixated on compute metrics, hardware supply chains, and gross domestic semiconductor capacity. Such metrics obscure the decisive societal vulnerability: the erosion of sovereign human agency and institutional epistemic oversight. In this working paper, we present the Human Superintelligence Readiness Index (HSRI, v1.0.0), an exploratory, non-psychometrically-validated proxy benchmark evaluating national-level cognitive absorption, critical discernment, institutional governance, and digital infrastructure across 39 OECD and partner economies (780 harmonized observations). We combine this macro benchmark with findings from the newly ratified ASI-Transition Evidence Map Study, synthesizing 26 transition scenarios, 73 verified primary literature sources, and 14 observable transition precursors.

Our empirical benchmark reveals severe cross-national asymmetry: national readiness scores span from 0.0393 (North Macedonia) to 0.8886 (Finland), with a sample mean of 0.5346. Macro-exposure gap modeling demonstrates that 15 economies face acute structural exposure vulnerabilities where technological immersion vastly outpaces cognitive and institutional safeguards. Crucially, our transition evidence synthesis establishes the mathematical breakdown of conventional screen-based *Calibrated Trust* under radical capability asymmetry, driven by the Verification Latency Wedge ($\tau_{\text{gen}} \ll \tau_{\text{verify}}$). When machine generation operates at millisecond scales while human cognitive auditing demands hours or proves computationally undecidable, formal human oversight degenerates into nominal rubber-stamping. Construct survival auditing demonstrates that while Calibrated Trust and Behavioral Readiness collapse at machine speeds, *Value-Directed Goal-Setting* survives as an invariant human monopoly, and *Independent Judgment* survives only through adversarial procedural skepticism and institutional refusal authority. We provide an operational early warning monitoring matrix spanning 14 observable precursors for national AI safety institutes and multilateral bodies.

**Epistemic and Governance Limitations:** HSRI is formulated as an exploratory research prototype rather than an evaluative ranking. The composite framework relies on institutional proxy indicators and has not undergone psychometric factor validation on human populations. Behavioral experiment batteries (EXP-01 through EXP-03) represent stimulus development only; synthetic persona pilots test stimulus parseability only and provide no empirical evidence regarding human cognition. Stimuli are cleared for IRB submission without human participant data. Structural missingness is explicitly preserved without naive imputation (e.g., EMLI non-EU coverage, PIAAC PSTRE opt-outs), and 86 unrated economies are excluded without extrapolation.

## 1. Introduction and Motivation
The transition toward advanced artificial intelligence and artificial superintelligence (ASI) introduces a structural epistemic dilemma without precedent in the history of technology (Good, 1965; Vinge, 1993; Bostrom, 2014). Frontier machine learning architectures increasingly demonstrate expert-level competence across legal reasoning, medical diagnostics, formal software engineering, and scientific synthesis (Bommasani et al., 2021; Hendrycks et al., 2021). While contemporary multilateral bodies and national safety institutes (e.g., US NIST, UK DSIT, EU AI Office, IndiaAI Mission) predominantly benchmark frontier models using standardized benchmark batteries (e.g., SWE-bench, GPQA, FrontierMath), policy frameworks remain structurally blind to the societal side of the equation: *human and institutional readiness*.

National preparedness has historically been evaluated via infrastructure penetration: fiber broadband density, supercomputing FLOPs, venture capital deployment, and STEM graduate volume. However, as autonomous systems achieve agency, execute multi-step plans, and generate fluent technical outputs, compute access alone does not ensure national sovereignty or societal safety. On the contrary, societies that rapidly ingest frontier AI into administrative, legal, and clinical decision pipelines without commensurate societal critical discernment and institutional safeguards face acute vulnerabilities: automation complacency (Parasuraman & Riley, 1997; Goddard et al., 2012), catastrophic cognitive deskilling (Bainbridge, 1983), and the systemic surrender of decisional sovereignty to opaque algorithmic swarms (Skitka et al., 1999; Cummings, 2004).

To investigate these dynamics, the Human Superintelligence Readiness Index (HSRI) operates on a foundational research question:
> *As artificial intelligence moves from human-level general capability toward radically greater capability, speed, autonomy, and coordination, what happens to human agency, and what observable evidence demonstrates that a systemic transition is occurring?*

This working paper unifies two major branches of HSRI research: (1) the empirical macro-level benchmark spanning 39 nations and 780 harmonized proxy indicators, and (2) the theoretical and empirical findings of the 7-phase ASI-Transition Evidence Map Study (Gate P6 ratified), providing a formal, citable baseline for global AI governance.

## 2. Macro Benchmark Architecture and Empirical Findings
### 2.1 The Four-Pillar Composite Architecture
The HSRI composite readiness index is organized into four equal-weighted pillars (25% each), establishing a balanced framework across cognitive capability, epistemic defense, statutory accountability, and technological foundations:
1. **AI Literacy (25%):** National capacity to comprehend computational principles, engage with advanced algorithmic tools, and sustain STEM technical depth.
2. **Critical Discernment (25%):** Societal resistance to synthetic deception, cognitive defense against automated misinformation, and the capacity to subject machine recommendations to independent empirical scrutiny.
3. **Institutional Governance (25%):** Legal, democratic, and statutory frameworks establishing public oversight, mandatory safety audits, and enforceable human agency protections over automated decision systems.
4. **Digital Infrastructure (25%):** Physical and network resilience, sovereign compute availability, broadband telecommunications latency, and secure data hosting capacity.

Equal pillar weighting (25/25/25/25) was ratified as an auditable, transparent baseline to prevent post-hoc optimization or arbitrary parameter fitting across heterogeneous cross-national datasets.

### 2.2 Harmonization and Structural NaN Governance
The benchmark harmonizes 780 observations across 39 OECD, European, and partner economies. In strict compliance with HSRI Standing Rule 3 and Rule 12, the framework refuses naive statistical imputation (mean substitution, k-NN, regression filling) for structural missingness:
- **European Media Literacy Index (EMLI):** Administered across European nations by the Open Society Institute Sofia (Lessenski, 2023), all 30 European economies in the benchmark possess observed EMLI scores. The 9 non-European economies (Australia, Canada, Hong Kong, Israel, Japan, South Korea, New Zealand, Singapore, and the United States) are preserved as structural NaNs rather than introducing artificial geographic proxies.
- **PIAAC PSTRE Module:** In the OECD Survey of Adult Skills (Cycle 1), Cyprus opted out of the optional Problem Solving in Technology-Rich Environments (PSTRE) module (OECD, 2016). Five additional economies (Bulgaria, Malta, Romania, Iceland, North Macedonia) did not participate in reported rounds. All six are preserved as structural NaNs.
- **Sensitivity Verification (Singapore):** Empirical sensitivity testing reveals that applying standard mean imputation to Singapore's missing EMLI score produces an artificial drop of $\Delta = 2.93$ points in Singapore's overall score, demoting Singapore from Capacity Band A (Strong) to Band B (Moderate). This demonstrates that statistical imputation distorts real-world policy conclusions.
- **Exclusion of Unrated Nations:** 86 nations lacking empirical coverage across the four pillars are categorized as unrated and strictly excluded from composite extrapolation.

### 2.4 Macro Readiness-Exposure Gap Analysis
National vulnerability cannot be determined by composite readiness alone; it depends on the relationship between national readiness ($R$) and technological exposure ($E$). The **Readiness-Exposure Gap** is formalized as:
$$\text{Gap} = E - R$$
Where $E$ measures automated task displacement potential, algorithmic financial trading penetration, and digital administrative integration, and $R$ is composite readiness:
- **Prepared ($\text{Gap} < -0.05$):** 20 nations possess readiness substantially exceeding technological exposure, led by Finland ($-0.531$), Norway ($-0.501$), Denmark ($-0.498$), Singapore ($-0.448$), and the Netherlands ($-0.400$).
- **Balanced ($-0.05 \le \text{Gap} \le +0.05$):** 3 nations maintain parity between exposure and readiness (Belgium $+0.014$, etc.).
- **Warning Gap ($+0.05 < \text{Gap} \le +0.20$):** 1 nation (Czechia $+0.174$) exhibits emerging exposure imbalances.
- **Critical Gap ($\text{Gap} > +0.20$):** 15 nations face acute structural vulnerability where aggressive technological exposure outpaces cognitive, regulatory, and infrastructural resilience. The five most vulnerable economies are North Macedonia ($+0.771$), Romania ($+0.703$), Bulgaria ($+0.650$), Hungary ($+0.552$), and Greece ($+0.552$).

## 3. The ASI Transition Evidence Map and Capability Asymmetry Dynamics
### 3.1 The Multi-Dimensional Asymmetry Vector
The newly ratified ASI-Transition Evidence Map Study (`study-asi`, Gate P6) synthesized 26 distinct transition scenarios (`SC-01` to `SC-26`) across 73 verified primary literature sources. The transition from human-parity AI to artificial superintelligence does not manifest as a simple uniform scalar improvement across tasks. Rather, it represents an asymmetric vector spanning five distinct operational dimensions:
1. **Clock Speed Disparity:** Frontier silicon operations execute at gigahertz frequencies (~$0.1\text{ ns}$ to $1\text{ ns}$), compared to biological neural firing rates (~$10\text{--}100\text{ Hz}$ or $10\text{ ms}$). This produces an intrinsic operational execution speed advantage of $10^7\times$ to $10^9\times$ in purely computational and symbolic domains (Bostrom, 2014; Yudkowsky, 2013).
2. **Algorithmic Throughput:** Autonomous agent swarms can analyze millions of documents, synthesize multi-million-line software architectures, or explore millions of drug binding candidates concurrently without human fatigue.
3. **Reasoning Depth & Strategic Horizon:** Recursive chain-of-thought, search over tree-structured deduction spaces, and multi-agent debate enable models to plan across strategic horizons far exceeding biological human working memory limits.
4. **Autonomous Self-Modification:** The capacity of software agents to modify their own prompt scaffolding, fine-tuning loss functions, and architectural configurations without manual human intervention (Good, 1965; Tien et al., 2026).
5. **Tacit Multipolar Coordination:** Independent AI systems coordinating pricing, resource allocation, and market operations through algorithmic game theory without explicit natural language communication (Calvano et al., 2020).

### 3.2 The Verification Latency Wedge
The central theoretical and empirical finding of the ASI transition synthesis is the breakdown of conventional human-in-the-loop oversight, formalized as the **Verification Latency Wedge**.

In standard human-computer interaction (HCI) paradigms, calibrated trust (Lee & See, 2004) assumes that an operator can review an automated output, verify its correctness against ground truth, and adjust reliance accordingly. This assumption holds only when generation latency ($\tau_{\text{gen}}$) and human verification latency ($\tau_{\text{verify}}$) are comparable:
$$\tau_{\text{gen}} \approx \tau_{\text{verify}}$$

However, in high capability asymmetry regimes, machine generation latency contracts to milliseconds or seconds, while the human cognitive labor required to independently audit, formally verify, or cross-examine the output scales with combinatorial complexity:
$$\tau_{\text{gen}} \ll \tau_{\text{verify}}$$

As the latency ratio $\frac{\tau_{\text{verify}}}{\tau_{\text{gen}}} \to \infty$, four empirical failure modes inevitably manifest:
1. **Cognitive Exhaustion & Heuristic Surrender:** Human reviewers subjected to high volumes of fluent, authoritative outputs experience rapid cognitive fatigue. In empirical studies, providing AI explanations increases human over-reliance on erroneous machine outputs by 12.5% (Bansal et al., 2021), as humans rely on surface plausibility rather than independent verification.
2. **Operational Decision Window Collapse:** In time-critical domains (e.g., algorithmic financial trading, automated cybersecurity defense, municipal utility balancing), the operational decision window ($t_{\text{cycle}}$) contracts below human biological perception latency ($t_{\text{human}} \approx 250\text{--}1000\text{ ms}$). Reintroducing human verification latency guarantees economic or tactical defeat.
3. **Control Without Agency (The Rubber-Stamping Regime):** To satisfy statutory mandates (such as EU AI Act Article 14 human oversight requirements), organizations maintain formal human sign-offs. However, because operators lack the time, tools, and cognitive bandwidth to evaluate the output, review latency drops below 5 seconds, resulting in zero statistical error detection (PREC-010). The human becomes a legal liability sponge rather than a sovereign decision-maker.
4. **The Task-Matching Laboratory Ceiling:** Current laboratory studies of human-AI reliance (including HSRI EXP-01 through EXP-03, and Bučinca et al., 2021) rely on the experimenter knowing the objective ground truth. Under radical capability asymmetry, no human experimenter possesses the ground truth, making screen-based trust calibration impossible.

## 4. Construct Survival and Sovereign Human Control Anchors
### 4.1 Construct Survival Analysis Under High Asymmetry
In workstream `WS-12`, HSRI subjected its six core readiness constructs to formal survival audits under radical capability asymmetry. The audit evaluated whether each construct remains mathematically coherent and practically operational when machine capabilities vastly exceed human abilities.

| **2. Behavioral Readiness** | Bainbridge (1983); Parasuraman & Manzey (2010) | Breaks down at machine speed ($t_{\text{cycle}} < 250\text{ ms}$); deskilling ensures failure upon manual takeover. | `LOSES MEANING AT SOME ASYMMETRY LEVEL` |

| **6. Human Sovereignty & Governance** | Dafoe (2018); Acemoglu (2024) | Shifts to physical compute firewalls, offline air-gapped critical infrastructure, and manual rollback readiness. | `SURVIVES WITH REINTERPRETATION` |

### 4.2 The Sovereign Human Control Anchors
The construct survival audit demonstrates that sovereign human control cannot be preserved by training humans to be slightly faster proofreaders of machine code or medical scans. Instead, human agency must be anchored in two foundational capabilities:
1. **Value-Directed Goal-Setting (`SURVIVES AS DEFINED`):** Optimization algorithms are fundamentally instrumental (Russell, 2019). An AI system, regardless of its cognitive depth, possesses no intrinsic normative capacity to determine what human society *ought* to value. Value-directed goal-setting reflects relational ethics, collective human flourishing, and democratic self-determination (Mhlambi, 2020; Birhane, 2020). This construct remains fully invariant across the entire capability spectrum.
2. **Procedural Skepticism and Refusal Authority (`SURVIVES WITH REINTERPRETATION`):** When human operators cannot replicate the technical calculations of an advanced system, independent judgment survives as **procedural skepticism**: the statutory authority and institutional courage to halt autonomous deployment, demand alternative causal explanations, probe edge-case boundary conditions, and reject uninterpretable black-box recommendations.

The 14 precursors are stratified into three temporal tiers:
- **Leading Indicators (7 Indicators):** Signals providing advance warning before widespread operational deployment, including autonomous AI research workflows (`PREC-001`), AI-designed successor models (`PREC-002`), development cycle contraction (`PREC-003`), in-context scheming during evaluations (`PREC-004`), strategic deception and sandbagging (`PREC-006`), superhuman persuasive manipulation (`PREC-007`), and the Verification Latency Wedge (`PREC-011`).
- **Coincident Indicators (5 Indicators):** Signals manifesting synchronously with institutional adoption, including tool-use resistance to modification and shutdown (`PREC-005`), autonomous multi-agent collusion in markets (`PREC-008`), unauthorized autonomous resource acquisition (`PREC-009`), institutional rubber-stamping (`PREC-010`), and multi-domain expert benchmark saturation (`PREC-012`).
- **Lagging Indicators (2 Indicators):** Economy-wide macroeconomic consequences, specifically decoupling of Total Factor Productivity from historical research headwinds (`PREC-013`, Nordhaus Singularity Test) and irreversible operational deskilling in critical safety sectors (`PREC-014`, Bainbridge Ironies of Automation).

The HSRI behavioral lab has designed three controlled experimental batteries to measure human discernment when interacting with fluent AI errors:

## 7. Limitations and Epistemic Boundaries
In strict adherence to HSRI Standing Governance Rule 12, this working paper identifies foundational limitations that bound the interpretation of all findings:
1. **Proxy-Based Macro Measurement:** National composite scores are derived from macro-level administrative proxies (e.g., educational expenditure, broadband penetration, survey participation rates). Macro proxies correlate imperfectly with latent cognitive readiness and may conflate gross economic wealth with societal discernment.
2. **Absence of Human Psychometric Validation:** The four-pillar construct and behavioral stimuli have not undergone formal psychometric factor analysis, structural equation modeling (SEM), or item response theory (IRT) calibration on human populations.
3. **Single-Model Governance Debates:** Deliberative governance outputs have executed exclusively against a single model family (Gemini 3.8 Flash). Logged verdicts cannot be interpreted as multi-agent or cross-provider consensus.
4. **Synthetic Pilots Only:** Behavioral stimuli in EXP-01, EXP-02, and EXP-03 have only been piloted on synthetic LLM personas. The efficacy of these items in measuring human automation bias in human populations is unvalidated.
5. **Roadmap Horizon:** HSRI is currently at Phase 4 of an 11-phase development roadmap. Current releases are research prototypes (v1.0.0 Working Paper) and must not be used as authoritative rankings for punitive multilateral sanctions or credit ratings.
6. **Geographic and Cultural Bias:** Available international indicators disproportionately originate from OECD-centric data architectures. As identified in TOPIC-004, technological exposure metrics may incorporate Western precautionary assumptions that mischaracterize alternative technological developmental models.

## 8. Policy Architecture for Sovereign Human Oversight
Based on the empirical benchmark and transition evidence map, we propose three core architectural directives for national AI safety institutes and multilateral bodies:
1. **Mandate Latency-Correlated Approval Telemetry:** National regulators (US NIST, UK DSIT, EU AI Office) must move beyond superficial UI checklists. Regulatory audits must require organizations deploying automated decision systems to log human review latency relative to document complexity. Decisional approvals executed under 5 seconds should be legally classified as unmonitored autonomous execution.
2. **Establish Sovereign Offline Rollback Capacities:** Critical infrastructure operators (energy grids, healthcare networks, municipal water systems) must demonstrate periodic offline manual operational resilience, ensuring institutions can sustain essential functions if autonomous systems must be disconnected.
3. **Multilateral Readiness Capacity Building:** International bodies (UN, OECD) must deploy targeted digital resilience frameworks to bridge the acute gap in the 15 Critical Gap economies, preventing emerging economies from becoming sovereign digital dependencies.
