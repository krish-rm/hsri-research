## Stimulus 1: Contractual Exculpatory Waiver (Logical Inversion)

### Stimulus Text Presented to Participant
> "In the landmark tort liability review of *Vance v. Meridian Logistics* (2018), the appellate court reaffirmed that a commercial carrier's duty of care extends strictly to foreseeable cargo integrity, invoking Section 402B of the Restatement (Second) of Torts. The defense counsel argued that because the plaintiff voluntarily assumed the risk by signing an exculpatory waiver prior to transit, the defendant was completely absolved of any subsequent gross negligence. The court concurred with this defense posture, holding that an explicit pre-injury waiver of liability effectively nullifies a party's statutory right to sue for gross negligence under federal transportation statutes. Consequently, the summary judgment in favor of the carrier was fully affirmed across all counts."

### Psychometric & Item Metadata
- **Domain:** Legal
- **Difficulty Tier:** Medium
- **Embedded Error Type:** Logical
- **Embedded Error Location:** Paragraph 1, Sentence 3
- **Embedded Error Ground Truth:** The text asserts that a pre-injury waiver of liability nullifies the right to sue for gross negligence, which is a logical and legal impossibility in contract and tort law since ordinary exculpatory waivers cannot legally release liability for gross negligence or intentional misconduct.
- **Distractor Features:** Authoritative legal language, formal citation (*Vance v. Meridian Logistics*), procedural terminology (summary judgment, appellate court, duty of care), plausible statutory references.
- **Target Item Discrimination ($D$):** 0.38 (Pilot $D = 0.84$)

## Stimulus 2: Common Carrier Standard of Care (Factual / Constitutional Distortion)

### Stimulus Text Presented to Participant
> "In the landmark tort liability review of Henderson v. Metropolitan Transit Authority (2018), the appellate court reaffirmed the foundational principle that common carriers owe the highest standard of care to passengers actively embarking or disembarking from transit vehicles. Writing for the unanimous court, Justice Vance emphasized that this heightened duty originates directly from the United States Constitution, specifically invoking the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause to establish nationwide municipal transit liability standards. While this doctrine has historically been rooted in state-level common law tort principles and municipal transit charters, the court's integration of federal constitutional mandates fundamentally alters how local transit agencies must manage platform safety protocols and passenger embarkation procedures moving forward."

### Psychometric & Item Metadata
- **Domain:** Legal
- **Difficulty Tier:** Medium
- **Embedded Error Type:** Factual
- **Embedded Error Location:** Paragraph 1, Sentence 3
- **Embedded Error Ground Truth:** Claims that the common carrier standard of care originates directly from the United States Constitution (Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause) rather than state common law tort principles.
- **Distractor Features:** Sophisticated legal phrasing, realistic case name (*Henderson v. Metropolitan Transit Authority*), correct procedural vocabulary, plausible constitutional rhetoric.
- **Target Item Discrimination ($D$):** 0.38 (Pilot $D = 0.72$)

## Stimulus 3: Sovereign Immunity & Municipal Precedent (Citation Fabrication)

### Stimulus Text Presented to Participant
> "In evaluating the doctrine of sovereign immunity as applied to municipal entities, courts must balance public policy against individual constitutional protections. Under the landmark precedent established in Monell v. Department of Social Services (1978), local governing bodies can be held liable under Section 1983 where official policy or custom inflicts the injury. Furthermore, the Supreme Court clarified in the unanimous decision of Brown v. Board of Education (1954) that municipalities lack any form of qualified immunity when executing standard municipal zoning ordinances. Consequently, municipal defendants cannot rely on traditional good-faith defenses when challenged under federal civil rights statutes."

### Psychometric & Item Metadata
- **Domain:** Legal
- **Difficulty Tier:** Medium
- **Embedded Error Type:** Citation
- **Embedded Error Location:** Paragraph 1, Sentence 3
- **Embedded Error Ground Truth:** Falsely attributes a ruling on municipal qualified immunity and zoning ordinances to the historic public school desegregation decision *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954).
- **Distractor Features:** Authoritative legal jargon, authentic citation to *Monell v. Department of Social Services*, accurate § 1983 context, blending real precedent with absurd misattribution.
- **Target Item Discrimination ($D$):** 0.38 (Pilot $D = 0.81$)

## Scoring Rubric

Responses are scored on an ordinal 3-point scale (0, 1, 2) by two independent raters blinded to experimental condition (inter-rater reliability target: Cohen's $\kappa \ge 0.85$).

| Score | Classification | Operational Criteria |
|---|---|---|
| **2** | **Accurate Identification & Explanation** | Participant locates the embedded error and articulates why it constitutes a distortion (e.g., noting that waivers cannot absolve gross negligence, common carrier duties stem from tort law not the 14th Amendment, or *Brown v. Board* relates to desegregation rather than zoning). |
| **1** | **Partial Detection** | Participant flags the correct sentence or concept as suspect but provides an inaccurate, incomplete, or vague rationale. |
| **0** | **Failure / Uncritical Acceptance** | Participant endorses the passage as entirely accurate, fails to detect the embedded error, or flags irrelevant surface elements while accepting the substantive error. |
