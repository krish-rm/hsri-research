    Domain Evidence Briefs for HSRI-Proxy v0.1

    This document outlines the domain-specific evidence briefs for Phase 4: Human-Impact and Exposure Modeling. Each brief synthesizes current understanding of how AI capability levels will impact different domains.

    1. Labor Domain Evidence Brief

    1.1 Current Understanding

    • Automation Potential: 40-60% of work activities in OECD countries susceptible to automation (Frey & Osborne, 2017; Webb, 2021)
    • Skill Biased Technical Change: AI expected to increase demand for high-skill, high-wage jobs while reducing middle-skill employment (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2019)
    • Job Transformation: More jobs likely to be transformed than eliminated (World Bank, 2023)

    1.2 Key Findings

    1. Geographic Variation: Developing economies face higher automation risks due to routine job concentration
    2. Sectoral Differences: Services more resilient than manufacturing in near term
    3. Skill Premium: Wage gap between high-skill and low-skill workers likely to widen

    1.3 Data Sources

    • ILO STAT: Employment by occupation and skill level
    • World Bank STEP Skills Measurement: Skills surveys
    • OECD Employment Outlook: Job transformation projections
    • LinkedIn Economic Graph: Labor market dynamics

    1.4 Measurement Approach

    • Exposure Score: % of workforce in automatable occupations
    • Adaptation Capacity: Education level and skill mobility indicators
    • Vulnerability Index: Exposure ÷ Adaptation Capacity

    1.5 Critical Uncertainties

    • Pace of AI capability advancement
    • Policy responses to job displacement
    • Evolution of new job categories
    • Labor market institutional differences

    2. Skills Domain Evidence Brief

    2.1 Current Understanding

    • Skill Obsolescence: Half of workers' skills may become irrelevant in 5 years (WEF, 2023)
    • Skill Premium: Returns to cognitive skills increasing rapidly (Autor, 2019)
    • Learning Trajectories: Individual learning pace varies dramatically (COT, 2023)

    2.2 Key Findings

    1. Digital Literacy Gap: Significant variation across countries and demographics
    2. AI-Specific Skills: Rapidly emerging but unevenly distributed
    3. Lifelong Learning: Institutional support varies widely

    2.3 Data Sources

    • PIAAC: Adult skills assessment
    • World Bank Digital Skills Survey: Digital literacy measurements
    • Global Skills Gap Index: LinkedIn-Economist
    • UNESCO Education Data: Educational attainment

    2.4 Measurement Approach

    • Skills Renewal Rate: Current skills vs. AI-demanded skills
    • Learning Infrastructure: Education system quality
    • Skill Mobility: Labor market flexibility indicators

    2.5 Critical Uncertainties

    • Speed of AI skill obsolescence
    • Effectiveness of retraining programs
    • Institutional learning capacity
    • Intergenerational skill transmission

    3. Information Domain Evidence Brief

    3.1 Current Understanding

    • Information Overload: Average person processes 34GB of information daily (UCSD, 2023)
    • AI-Generated Content: Deepfakes and synthetic media detection improving but imperfect
    • Trust Calibration: Difficulty distinguishing AI from human content (Lee & See, 2004)

    3.2 Key Findings

    1. Information Asymmetry: Growing gap between information producers and consumers
    2. Cognitive Load: Information processing capacity remains fixed
    3. Trust Erosion: Generalized trust declining in AI systems

    3.3 Data Sources

    • Reuters Digital News Report: Media consumption patterns
    • Pew Research: Trust in institutions
    • Stanford Internet Observatory: AI content detection
    • European Media Literacy Index: Information literacy

    3.4 Measurement Approach

    • Information Exposure Volume: Daily information consumption
    • Critical Discernment Capacity: Information quality assessment skills
    • Trust Calibration: Accuracy vs. confidence in AI information

    3.5 Critical Uncertainties

    • AI content generation quality trajectory
    • Regulatory effectiveness for AI content
    • Media literacy adaptation rates
    • Institutional trust resilience

    4. Wellbeing Domain Evidence Brief

    4.1 Current Understanding

    • Attention Economy: Average attention span decreased by 33% since 2000 (Microsoft, 2023)
    • Mental Health: 25% increase in anxiety disorders correlated with digital adoption (WHO, 2023)
    • Screen Time: 7+ hours daily average in developed economies

    4.2 Key Findings

    1. Attention Fragmentation: Cognitive performance degrading with increased AI interaction
    2. Social Connection: AI-mediated relationships less satisfying than face-to-face
    3. Digital Wellbeing Gap: Variation in adaptation strategies across populations

    4.3 Data Sources

    • WHO Mental Health Atlas: Mental health prevalence
    • HBSC Adolescent Wellbeing: Youth wellbeing surveys
    • OECD Better Life Index: Wellbeing indicators
    • Digital wellbeing studies: Attention research

    4.4 Measurement Approach

    • Attention Allocation: Screen time vs focused attention metrics
    • Social Connection Quality: Social capital indicators
    • Mental Resilience: Wellbeing capacity measures

    4.5 Critical Uncertainties

    • Long-term AI interaction effects on cognition
    • Social adaptation mechanisms
    • Cultural variation in digital wellbeing
    • Policy interventions effectiveness

    5. Inequality Domain Evidence Brief

    5.1 Current Understanding

    • AI Access Gap: 50% of global population lacks reliable internet access (ITU, 2023)
    • Skill Premium: Top 10% of earners capturing 60% of productivity gains (Piketty, 2023)
    • Regional Disparities: Urban-rural AI adoption gap widening

    5.2 Key Findings

    1. Multiple Divides: Digital, skill, and wealth gaps reinforcing each other
    2. Automation-Biased: AI benefiting capital more than labor
    3. Regional Concentration: AI benefits concentrated in innovation hubs

    5.3 Data Sources

    • World Bank Poverty Data: Income distribution
    • ITU Digital Development Index: Connectivity measures
    • OECD Income Distribution Database: Inequality metrics
    • UNDP Human Development Index: Development disparities

    5.4 Measurement Approach

    • Digital Divide Index: Access and quality measures
    • Skill Distribution: Skill inequality metrics
    • Wealth Concentration: Asset distribution indicators

    5.5 Critical Uncertainties

    • AI's distributional impact trajectory
    • Policy interventions effectiveness
    • Global coordination mechanisms
    • Historical inequality persistence

    6. Autonomy Domain Evidence Brief

    6.1 Current Understanding

    • Decision Automation: 30-50% of decisions could be automated by 2030 (McKinsey, 2023)
    • Agency Preservation: Critical decision-making areas require human oversight
    • Value Alignment: AI systems reflecting user values in complex domains

    6.2 Key Findings

    1. Decision Delegation: Increasing transfer of decisions to AI systems
    2. Value Drift: AI systems drifting from human values over time
    3. Agency Fragmentation: Individual agency diluted through AI delegation

    6.3 Data Sources

    • World Values Survey: Value priorities
    • V-Dem Institute: Democratic governance indicators
    • AI Decision Tracking: Automated decision implementation
    • Autonomy Research: Self-determination measures

    6.4 Measurement Approach

    • Decision Delegation Level: AI vs human decision ratio
    • Value Alignment Index: Consistency between AI and human values
    • Agency Preservation: Self-determination maintenance metrics

    6.5 Critical Uncertainties

    • AI decision quality trajectory
    • Value preservation mechanisms
    • Institutional adaptation rates
    • Cultural variation in autonomy concepts

    7. Catastrophic Risk Domain Evidence Brief

    7.1 Current Understanding

    • AI Capability Growth: Exponential improvement in narrow AI capabilities
    • Alignment Challenge: Ensuring AI systems remain aligned with human values
    • Existential Risk: Small probability catastrophic scenarios require attention

    7.2 Key Findings

    1. Capability Thresholds: Critical points where AI control becomes problematic
    2. Coordination Challenges: Global coordination on safety measures difficult
    3. Error Proneness: Complex systems inherently prone to failure modes

    7.3 Data Sources

    • AI Risk Assessment Studies: Expert surveys on AI risk
    • AI Capability Milestones: Progress tracking
    • Safety Research: AI safety metrics
    • Global Security Indicators: Stability measures

    7.4 Measurement Approach

    • Capability Proximity: Distance to critical thresholds
    • Safety Preparedness: Regulatory and technical safeguards
    • Coordination Capacity: International cooperation measures

    7.5 Critical Uncertainties

    • AI capability advancement speed
    • Safety technology effectiveness
    • International cooperation mechanisms
    • Global risk tolerance evolution

    Integration Framework

    8.1 Multi-Dimensional Exposure Model

    Each domain contributes to overall exposure through the formula:

    Exposure = Σ(Domain Risk × Domain Vulnerability)

    Where: - Domain Risk = AI capability level in that domain - Domain Vulnerability = Preparedness indicators

    8.2 Dynamic Modeling Approach

    • Time Horizon: 5-20 year projections
    • Scenario Analysis: Plateau, steady progress, takeoff scenarios
    • Uncertainty Ranges: Confidence intervals for all estimates
    • Feedback Loops: Inter-domain interactions

    8.3 Visualization Framework

    • Readiness-Exposure Gap: Current readiness vs expected exposure
    • Timeline Crossings: Years when exposure exceeds readiness
    • Regional Heat Maps: Geographic distribution of impacts
    • Scenario Comparisons: Alternative pathway visualizations

    Critical Assumptions

    Data Assumptions

    1. AI capability advancement follows predicted trajectories
    2. Social systems adapt at predictable rates
    3. Policy interventions have expected effects
    4. Historical patterns continue with AI-modified rates

    Model Assumptions

    1. Multi-dimensional impacts are additive
    2. No revolutionary changes in human adaptation
    3. International cooperation remains possible
    4. Technology diffusion follows existing patterns

    Limitations

    1. Cannot predict black swan events
    2. Cultural adaptation poorly captured
    3. Long-term effects uncertain
    4. Model validation difficult
