## 1. Executive Summary: The Emergence of the Asymmetric Readiness Divide

International deliberations on AI governance predominantly emphasize universal ethical principles, AI safety summits, and voluntary corporate codes of conduct. While these initiatives are constructive, they frequently proceed on an unexamined assumption: that all sovereign nations possess comparable structural capacities to withstand and govern the societal absorption of frontier artificial intelligence.

The empirical findings of the **Human Superintelligence Readiness Index (HSRI, v1.0.0)**, evaluating 780 harmonized observations across 39 nations, disprove this assumption.

The data reveals an acute **Macro Readiness-Exposure Divide**:
- **20 Economies are "Prepared":** Concentrated in Northern and Western Europe and advanced Asian economies (e.g., Finland $-0.531$, Norway $-0.501$, Denmark $-0.498$, Singapore $-0.448$, Netherlands $-0.400$), where composite societal readiness substantially exceeds technological exposure.
- **15 Economies Face a "Critical Gap":** Primarily middle-income, Southern, and Eastern European economies (led by North Macedonia $+0.771$, Romania $+0.703$, Bulgaria $+0.650$, Hungary $+0.552$, Greece $+0.552$), where aggressive exposure to automated systems outpaces institutional, educational, and infrastructural safeguards.
- **86 Developing Economies Remain Completely Unrated:** Lacking empirical baseline data across core cognitive and institutional indicators, these nations face acute risks of invisible technological dependency.

Without coordinated multilateral intervention, advanced AI will not act as a global leveler. Instead, it will create an irreversible regime of **technological clientelism**, where unready societies become dependent consumers of external cognitive monopolies while suffering rapid domestic workforce and institutional displacement.

## 2. Empirical Exposure-Readiness Disparities in the HSRI Dataset

In the HSRI framework, vulnerability is quantified by the **Readiness-Exposure Gap** ($	ext{Gap} = 	ext{Exposure} - 	ext{Readiness}$):

### Core Empirical Drivers of the Critical Gap:
1. **Critical Discernment Deficit:** While digital infrastructure (broadband, 5G) has diffused rapidly into emerging markets, societal critical discernment (resilience against synthetic disinformation, algorithmic media literacy) has lagged severely. In limited capacity nations, media literacy scores average below $0.15$ (Lessenski 2023).
2. **Institutional Governance Asymmetry:** Advanced economies have enacted comprehensive statutory protections, independent oversight boards, and national AI safety institutes. In contrast, Critical Gap economies lack dedicated algorithmic audit capabilities, leaving public procurement vulnerable to unvetted corporate solutions.
3. **The Non-OECD Missingness Trap:** Over 86 global South and developing economies lack participation in standardized assessments (PIAAC, EMLI). Treating missingness with naive statistical imputation masks systemic underinvestment. HSRI strictly refuses imputation to prevent policy complacency.

## 3. The Threat of Technological Clientelism and Cognitive Extraction

When a nation with low institutional readiness experiences high technological exposure, three structural hazards emerge:

1. **Epistemic Offloading & Generational Deskilling (PREC-014):** When local educational systems and professional workforces offload diagnostic and analytical functions to foreign foundation models, domestic problem-solving capabilities atrophy non-linearly (Bainbridge 1983). Over a 5-to-10-year horizon, the nation loses the human capital necessary to audit or replace foreign software.
2. **Compute Dependency & Loss of Policy Sovereignty:** Economies without sovereign domestic compute or open-source infrastructure must route critical governmental, economic, and security telemetry through foreign hyperscaler datacenters, exposing sovereign policy to external terms-of-service manipulation.
3. **Algorithmic Labor Market Arbitrage:** As frontier models automate white-collar cognitive labor (Bostrom 2014; Acemoglu 2024), middle-income economies whose development model relies on business process outsourcing (BPO), legal services, and software contracting face severe economic displacement without the capital buffers of advanced economies.

## 4. Multilateral Action Agenda for the UN and OECD

We propose a four-point multilateral action agenda for the UN High-Level Advisory Body on AI and the OECD AI Policy Observatory:

### 1. Establish the "Global AI Readiness Facility" (GAIR-F)
Under the auspices of the UN Tech Envoy and ITU, establish a dedicated multilateral capacity-building fund targeting the 15 Critical Gap economies and unrated developing nations. GAIR-F should fund:
- Independent national statutory audit boards.
- Open-access public sector compute infrastructure.
- Standardized cross-cultural human discernment benchmarking.

### 2. Standardize Public Algorithmic Registries
Adopt an OECD-wide binding standard requiring member states to maintain public registries of all automated decision systems deployed in public administration, including mandatory logging of human review latency and override metrics (addressing PREC-010).

### 3. Reform Global Digital Missingness Protocols
Direct UNESCO and the World Bank to fund localized, culturally adapted cognitive and AI discernment assessments across the 86 currently unrated nations, eliminating the geographic blind spots in current global data architectures.

### 4. Create an International Non-Proliferation Compact for Autonomous Coercion
Establish multilateral verification protocols monitoring the 14 HSRI transition precursors (`PREC-001` to `PREC-014`), ensuring that dangerous autonomous agent capabilities (such as autonomous compute acquisition, PREC-009, and covert scheming, PREC-004) are subject to international inspection treaties analogous to the IAEA framework.
